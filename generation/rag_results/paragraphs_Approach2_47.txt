LVD (PVE with HVE) followed by major hepa\u00ad\ntectomy is a new technique for improving liver regeneration.\nKobayashi et al. [37] reported the safety and greater efficacy of\nLVD in inducing FLR hypertrophy compared with PVE alone. Al\u00ad\nthough it is generally accepted that LVD can be performed when a\nhigher degree of FLR hypertrophy is desired, there are also con\u00ad\nflicting reports that state that LVD provides no significant ad\u00ad\nvantage over PVE alone [38,39]. As the evidence for LVD vs PVE is\nbased on retrospective studies, the results of the DRAGON 1 trial\n(safety and feasibility) and the DRAGON 2 trial are eagerly\nawaited [40,41].\n2. TSH: The concept of TSH was introduced by Adam et al. [42] in\n1992 for extensive bilobar CRLM. The indication for TSH is bilobar\nCRLM with the largest CRLM of > 3 cm and/or > 3 lesions in the\nFLR with an FLR of > 30% [43]. A typical TSH sequential treatment\nbegins with first-stage liver resection, which involves clearing the\nFLR of the disease. This is generally followed by PVE 2 to 5 weeks\nlater to induce FLR hypertrophy. Another feasible alternative is\nintraoperative PVL or PVE during the first stage to minimize the\ntime interval during first- and second-stage hepatectomy and\nreduce patient dropout. The MD Anderson Cancer Center has\npioneered the \u201cfast track\u201d TSH approach in which a combined\nfirst-stage hepatectomy and PVE is performed in the same setting\nin a hybrid interventional radiology/operative room suite [44].\n\nThis is followed by a second-stage major hepatectomy (typically\nright or extended right). Interval chemotherapy can be adminis\u00ad\ntered between the 2 surgical stages, but it is not routinely fa\u00ad\nvored. Of note, a 5-year OS of near 50% was achieved in patients\ndeemed as initially unresectable after TSH combined with ef\u00ad\nfective systemic chemotherapy [45,46]. In synchronous pre\u00ad\nsentations, the primary tumor can be resected during the first\nstage of surgery.\n3. ALPPS: In 2012, Eduardo de Santiba\u00f1es and Pierre-Alain Clavien\ncoined the self-explanatory term \u201cALPPS\u201d [47]. In the following\ndecade, further refinement and modifications have occurred in\nthe ALPPS technique (hybrid ALPPS, tourniquet ALPPS, etc.),\nwhich is beyond the scope of this review. Strictly speaking, ALPPS\nis a TSH variant that induces larger and faster FLR hypertrophy\nand reduces the time duration between the 2 surgical stages.\nOver and above the indications for TSH, ALPPS is used in cases of\nCRLM as a last resort when other surgical modalities in the ar\u00ad\nmamentarium are deemed inappropriate in cases in which\nmultiple lesions are in close contact with FLR and its vascular\npedicle, failure of PVE/PVL, and, most importantly, when a large\nhypertrophy is needed [43]. Although ALPPS is feasible and has\nadvantages, perioperative complications (including mortality)\nand lack of data on long-term outcomes remain a concern. Be\u00ad\ncause of these concerns, coupled with the advent of parenchymasparing surgery, R1 vascular surgery, and liver resections with\nvascular reconstruction, the use of ALPPS needs to be further\nrevisited in BR-CRLM before its widespread use. However, an RCT\nfrom a North European group evaluated the outcome between\nALPPS and TSH and showed that the feasibility of ALPPS was\nsuperior to TSH, with comparable morbidity and mortality\nrates [48].\n4. One-stage hepatectomy (OSH)/parenchymal sparing surgery\n(PSS): Multiple partial liver resections are performed as a single\nprocedure to remove all the liver lesions, which is commonly\nadopted for superficial lesions (cherry-picking surgery). Less\ncommonly, this procedure can be performed even for more\ncomplex procedures involving right hepatectomy with PSS for\nleft-sided lesions with or without PVE. Torzilli et al. [49,50] ex\u00ad\ntended the PSS to deep-seated lesions (including those in contact\nwith major intrahepatic vessels) and introduced the concept of\n\u201cextended OSH,\u201d thereby using PVE only in exceptional situa\u00ad\ntions. Imperative technical prerequisites include the use of in\u00ad\ntraoperative ultrasound to guide parenchymal resection. Vascular\ncontact with major blood vessels defines marginally resectable\nCRLM, a subset of BR-CRLM. Once objective response is achieved\nby systemic chemotherapy, liver surgeons are left with 2 options:\nR1 resection by necessity [51] or liver resection with vascular\nreconstruction. R1 resection because of vascular contact is fea\u00ad\nsible and may achieve results comparable with R0 resection,\nwhich is not the case for parenchymal R1 resection [52]. This\nfinding may be dependent on tumor biology, particularly _KRAS_\nmutation [53]. Various parenchymal-sparing techniques that\nemploy R1 vascular resection have been described with accep\u00ad\ntable oncologically outcomes [54]. Some preoperative parameters\nmay lead surgeons to prefer radical resections with vascular re\u00ad\nconstruction rather than PSS (eg, contact of > 25% of the vessel\ncircumference, which is associated with a higher risk of vessel\ninvasion) [55]. However, a lack of technical expertise and a higher\nrisk of postoperative morbidity, including posthepatectomy liver\nfailure, are deterrents to its widespread applicability.\n5. HAIC has the potential to act synergistically with systemic\nchemotherapy to achieve resectability in cases of initially\nunresectable CRLM. The primary endpoint of the OPTILIV trial\nevaluating the incidence of complete macroscopic resections of liver\nmetastases was met when R0-R1 hepatectomy was achieved in 19 of\n64 patients (29.7%) with initially unresectable CRLM presurgically\ntreated with HAIC. Those with R0-R1 hepatectomy had a median OS of\n35.2 months (IQR, 32.6\u201337.8), with 37.4% of patients (23.6%\u221251.2%)\nalive at the end of 4 years [57]. In 2013, a study from Memorial Sloan\nKettering Cancer Centre (MSKCC) summarized their experience of\nusing HAIC to treat unresectable liver metastases. Of the 373 patients\nincluded, 93 (25.0%) eventually underwent complete resection and/or\nablation, resulting in a median survival of 59 months [58]. The\nsuccessful resectability achieved in this retrospective study was\nfurther replicated in prospective trials conducted by the same\ncenter using strict definitions to categorize patients as initially\nunresectable CRLM [59,60]. There are currently enough data to\nsupport the use of HAIC along with systemic therapy to produce\nresponse rates higher than systemic chemotherapy alone. For patients\nwho have failed multiple lines of chemotherapy, HAIC has shown\nbenefit in terms of achieving superior response rates. Another role of\nHAIC in patients with BR-CRLM is in the adjuvant setting after a\nsuccessful hepatic resection to decrease the incidence of recurrence in\nthe remnant liver. Before instituting HAIC, surgical placement of a\nhepatic artery infusion (HAI) catheter and pump is required,\ncommonly in the gastroduodenal artery after delineating the arterial\nanatomy of the liver via cross-sectional imaging. Complications\nassociated with HAI pump placement are primarily related to the\ncatheter, such as arterial thrombosis, whereas systemic complications\nassociated with extrahepatic perfusion are rare.\n\n_TACE_ . TACE is a widely used and accepted treatment modality in\nunresectable hepatocellular carcinoma. Conventional TACE and its\nrelatively newer technology of drug-eluting beads (DEB-TACE)\nconsisting of a novel drug delivery system have been investigated in\nCRLM. After its successful application in the chemotherapy-refractory\nunresectable CRLM, its utility was explored in the neoadjuvant setting\nto achieve resectability in patients who were deemed as initially\nunresectable. A multicenter RCT comparing DEB-TACE with irinotecan\nbeads (DEBIRI-TACE) with systemic chemotherapy with systemic\nchemotherapy alone in unresectable CRLM found higher response\nrates, longer PFS (15.2 vs 7.6, respectively), and greater conversion to\nhepatic resection in the combination group [61]. The PARAGON II trial\nexplored the role of DEBIRI-TACE for potentially resectable CRLM. Of 40\npatients recruited in the trial who underwent preoperative DEBIRITACE, 38 (95.0%) underwent resection with an R0 resection rate of\n74.0% [62]. Nonetheless, this technique should still be considered as a\nsecondary treatment option in CRLM and more so in BR-CRLM.\n\n_Ablation techniques_\nRFA, MWA, and the relatively novel technique of irreversible\nelectroporation (IRE) are the ablation modalities tried and tested in\nCRLM. A major limitation of RFA is the heat sink effect, which occurs\nwhen a tumor abuts a blood vessel of a size of > 3 mm. This limita\u00ad\ntion is somewhat circumvented with MWA, which produces more\nheat. However, IRE is not impeded by the heat sink effect of neigh\u00ad\nboring blood vessels. The ablation of tumor size up to 3 cm has\nshown to be equally effective as resection in terms of oncologically\noutcomes [63,64]. For tumor sizes of 3 to 5 cm, ablation can be\n\nconsidered by overlapping multiple approaches to achieve complete\ntumor coverage. Ablation is generally not recommended for a tumor\nsize larger than 5 cm [65]. The role of ablation in BR-CRLM is in\npatients with bilobar lesions in which complete R0 resection cannot\nbe achieved without adequate FLR. Hepatectomy combined with\nintraoperative or postoperative ablation can achieve acceptable re\u00ad\nsults as R0 resection by preserving adequate FLR [66,67]. A study\ncomparing OSH with ablation vs TSH or hepatectomy alone in CRLM\ndid not find any difference in the 5-year OS [66]. For patients pre\u00ad\nsenting with postresection recurrence, image-guided ablation pro\u00ad\nvides an effective alternative strategy to liver resection [68]. Multiple\nsessions of ablation can be performed with less destruction of\nnormal liver parenchyma, resulting in survival similar to patients\nwithout recurrence [69]. The results of the MAVERRIC and COLLIS\u00ad\nION trials have shown that thermal ablation is an equally valid\ncurative treatment modality compared with surgery for a CRLM of\n\u22643 cm [70,71].\n\n_RT_\nLocal regional treatment with RT consists of conventional RT,\nstereotactic beam RT (SBRT), TARE, and brachytherapy. The roles of\nSBRT and TARE in patients with CRLM who are not surgical candi\u00ad\ndates (unresectable) and/or who have failed first- or second-line\nchemotherapy have been described. SBRT has a complementary role\nwith other locoregional therapies, such as ablation. A recent metaanalysis concluded that SBRT for liver oligometastases is an effective\ntreatment option for patients with advanced colorectal cancer, en\u00ad\ncouraging local control and survival [72]. However, the role of SBRT\nas a downstaging strategy to achieve resectability has not yet elu\u00ad\ncidated and currently remains only as an effective alternative\ntreatment strategy for patients who are not suitable surgical can\u00ad\ndidates. TARE using yttrium-90 (Y-90), a type of selective intra-ar\u00ad\nterial radiotherapy, is a therapeutic alternative for selected patients\nwith CRLM. Although RCTs provide no to minimal support for adding\nTARE as an adjunct to second-line chemotherapy (EPOCH trial) or in\nthe first-line setting (FOXFIRE, SIR-FLOX, and FOXFIRE-Global trials),\nthe role of TARE in BR-CRLM is still evolving [73]. As a part of their\nevidence-based expert consensus treatment algorithm, Jeyarajah\net al. [10] described the role of Y-90 TARE in BR-CRLM. TARE has a\nrole in presurgical treatment in patients with BR-CRLM who have\nbeen subjected to systemic chemotherapy but have inadequate\nradiologic response, precluding a safe resection without adequate\nFLR, or in patients who remain unresectable after systemic che\u00ad\nmotherapy [10]. Radiation segmentectomy/lobectomy is a promising\ntechnique that uses ablative technique for patients who are not\nsurgical candidates and tumors not amenable to ablation. There is\nemerging evidence that TARE can be used to induce FLR and has a\nrole in achieving resectability when used along with first-line che\u00ad\nmotherapy in patients with unresectable CRLM [74].\n\n_Role of the multidisciplinary liver tumor board_\n\nAs there is no uniform definition of BR-CRLM, treatment deci\u00ad\nsions have to be individualized, and a patient-centric approach\nneeds to be adopted to obtain a \u201ctailor-made\u201d treatment strategy\n(Figs. 2 and 3).
