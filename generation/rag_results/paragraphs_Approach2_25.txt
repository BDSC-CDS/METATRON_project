The purpose of this study\nis to evaluate the conversion rate from unresectable CRLM to complete resection.\nMethods We conducted a multi-institutional phase II trial for unresectable CRLM. Patients\nreceived mFOLFOX6 with either bevacizumab (bev) or cetuximab (cet) based on K-ras status\n(UMIN000004310). Anti-EGFR therapy should\nbe limited to patients with wild-type RAS and BRAF, whereas\nanti-VEGF therapy has no restriction for its use in terms of bio\u00ad\nlogic molecular profile. A meta-analysis reported that anti-EGFR\ntherapy can increase the R0 resection rate by up to 60% in pa\u00ad\ntients with metastatic colorectal cancer and unresectable liverlimited disease [28].\n2. The optimal duration of chemotherapy: Conversion che\u00ad\nmotherapy aims to achieve resectability and not complete pa\u00ad\nthologic response. Prolongation of chemotherapy is detrimental\nfor the following reasons [29]:\n\ni. The greater the number of cycles administered before surgery,\nthe higher the chance of chemotherapy-associated liver injury\n(eg, \u201cblue liver\u201d and \u201cyellow liver\u201d associated with prolonged\nusage of oxaliplatin and irinotecan, respectively).\nii. Prolonged chemotherapy may result in the development of\n\u201cdisappearing liver metastasis (DLM),\u201d especially in relatively\nsmaller unresectable CRLM, which pose a very challenging clin\u00ad\nical scenario. The placement of a fiducial marker before starting\nchemotherapy has been proposed to guide resection after the\ncompletion of chemotherapy. Thus, to avoid these difficult si\u00ad\ntuations, tumor response needs to be evaluated every 2 months.\nIt is recommended to give at least 4 courses of first-line che\u00ad\nmotherapy (2 months) before the first evaluation of tumor re\u00ad\nsponse. If the intended downsizing is not sufficient, another 4\ncourses can be administered. In cases in which there is stable\ndisease or progression after 4 months of chemotherapy, secondline chemotherapy with an intent to \u201csalvage/convert\u201d should be\ninstituted. It should be noted that the total duration of perio\u00ad\nperative chemotherapy of 6 months is recommended.\niii. Lastly, the higher the waiting time to achieve a response, the\nhigher the chances of tumor progression and dissemination\nwithin and outside the liver because of acquired resistance to\nchemotherapy. Thus, unnecessary prolongation is not justified, as\nit is a known fact that survival benefit after hepatic resection is\nmore only for patients who achieve downsizing than for those\nwho progress on chemotherapy.\n\nIt is worthwhile to discuss the management of 2 important\nclinical situations often encountered in clinical practice while\nmanaging patients with BR-CRLM undergoing conversion che\u00ad\nmotherapy.\n\n1. Progression on first-line chemotherapy: In such situations, the\naim is to start second-line chemotherapy with an intention for\nconversion to resectability. Hepatic resection after conversion to\nsecond-line chemotherapy is believed to achieve the same result\nin terms of survival benefit as those after conversion to first-line\n\n4\n\n_G.P. Varty et al._ _Journal of Gastrointestinal Surgery 29 (2025) 101868_\n\nchemotherapy [30]. Another option is the administration of HAIC\nin these patients as an effective strategy to downsize. Upfront\nsurgery after progression to first-line chemotherapy is also an\noption, but it yields dismal results (3-year OS of < 10%) in the\npresence of poor prognostic factors, such as the largest CRLM\ndiameter of \u226550 mm, number of CRLMs of \u22653, and/or CEA of\n\u2265200 ng/mL [31].\n2. DLM: DLMs are defined as the complete response or dis\u00ad\nappearance of CRLM after chemotherapy on cross-sectional\nimaging studies. Small size (< 2 cm), increased number of che\u00ad\nmotherapy cycles, oxaliplatin-based therapy, number of CRLM of\n\u22653, and synchronous CRLM are the predisposing factors. The\nplacement of fiducial markers before chemotherapy helps iden\u00ad\ntify DLM during surgery or ablation [32,33]. Although the resec\u00ad\ntion of DLM sites is associated with the least intrahepatic\nrecurrence, the management of DLM should be guided by a\nmultidisciplinary team approach, as treatment needs to be highly\nindividualized, consisting of surgery, additional systemic/local\nregional therapies, or close surveillance [34].\n\n_Surgery for BR-CRLM: pushing the limits to achieve resectability_\n\nConcurrently, along with the evolution of effective che\u00ad\nmotherapy, there have been great strides in the development of\nadvanced surgical techniques to achieve resectability in BR-CRLM\nwith the preservation/augmentation of adequate FLR.\n\n1. PVE: It was first used in bilateral CRLM by Kawasaki et al. [35] in\n1994. The indications for performing PVE are an FLR cutoff of\n< 25% to 30% for a normal liver or 35% to 40% for a diseased liver\n(chemotherapy-associated steatohepatitis or cholestatic liver). A\nsystematic review of 1791 patients showed that the mean in\u00ad\ncrease in FLR volume was 37.9% \u00b1 0.1% (range, 20.5%\u221269.4%) over\na mean time interval of 25.9 \u00b1 10.1 days (range, 14.0-42.0 days).\nAfter PVE, more than 70% of patients could undergo a right he\u00ad\npatectomy or extended right hepatectomy on an average of 36.9\ndays (range, 21.0-84.0 days) after PVE. The overall morbidity and\nmortality after liver resection are reported to be 21.7% and 3.3%,\nrespectively [36]. Of note, 1 main drawback of PVE is the risk of\ntumor progression, particularly in bilobar CRLM during the\nwaiting period. LVD (PVE with HVE) followed by major hepa\u00ad\ntectomy is a new technique for improving liver regeneration.\nKobayashi et al. [37] reported the safety and greater efficacy of\nLVD in inducing FLR hypertrophy compared with PVE alone. Al\u00ad\nthough it is generally accepted that LVD can be performed when a\nhigher degree of FLR hypertrophy is desired, there are also con\u00ad\nflicting reports that state that LVD provides no significant ad\u00ad\nvantage over PVE alone [38,39]. As the evidence for LVD vs PVE is\nbased on retrospective studies, the results of the DRAGON 1 trial\n(safety and feasibility) and the DRAGON 2 trial are eagerly\nawaited [40,41].\n2. TSH: The concept of TSH was introduced by Adam et al. [42] in\n1992 for extensive bilobar CRLM. The indication for TSH is bilobar\nCRLM with the largest CRLM of > 3 cm and/or > 3 lesions in the\nFLR with an FLR of > 30% [43]. A typical TSH sequential treatment\nbegins with first-stage liver resection, which involves clearing the\nFLR of the disease. This is generally followed by PVE 2 to 5 weeks\nlater to induce FLR hypertrophy. Another feasible alternative is\nintraoperative PVL or PVE during the first stage to minimize the\ntime interval during first- and second-stage hepatectomy and\nreduce patient dropout. The MD Anderson Cancer Center has\npioneered the \u201cfast track\u201d TSH approach in which a combined\nfirst-stage hepatectomy and PVE is performed in the same setting\nin a hybrid interventional radiology/operative room suite [44].\n\nThis is followed by a second-stage major hepatectomy (typically\nright or extended right). Interval chemotherapy can be adminis\u00ad\ntered between the 2 surgical stages, but it is not routinely fa\u00ad\nvored. Of note, a 5-year OS of near 50% was achieved in patients\ndeemed as initially unresectable after TSH combined with ef\u00ad\nfective systemic chemotherapy [45,46]. In synchronous pre\u00ad\nsentations, the primary tumor can be resected during the first\nstage of surgery.\n3. ALPPS: In 2012, Eduardo de Santiba\u00f1es and Pierre-Alain Clavien\ncoined the self-explanatory term \u201cALPPS\u201d [47]. In the following\ndecade, further refinement and modifications have occurred in\nthe ALPPS technique (hybrid ALPPS, tourniquet ALPPS, etc.),\nwhich is beyond the scope of this review. Strictly speaking, ALPPS\nis a TSH variant that induces larger and faster FLR hypertrophy\nand reduces the time duration between the 2 surgical stages.\nOver and above the indications for TSH, ALPPS is used in cases of\nCRLM as a last resort when other surgical modalities in the ar\u00ad\nmamentarium are deemed inappropriate in cases in which\nmultiple lesions are in close contact with FLR and its vascular\npedicle, failure of PVE/PVL, and, most importantly, when a large\nhypertrophy is needed [43]. Although ALPPS is feasible and has\nadvantages, perioperative complications (including mortality)\nand lack of data on long-term outcomes remain a concern. Be\u00ad\ncause of these concerns, coupled with the advent of parenchymasparing surgery, R1 vascular surgery, and liver resections with\nvascular reconstruction, the use of ALPPS needs to be further\nrevisited in BR-CRLM before its widespread use. However, an RCT\nfrom a North European group evaluated the outcome between\nALPPS and TSH and showed that the feasibility of ALPPS was\nsuperior to TSH, with comparable morbidity and mortality\nrates [48].\n4. One-stage hepatectomy (OSH)/parenchymal sparing surgery\n(PSS): Multiple partial liver resections are performed as a single\nprocedure to remove all the liver lesions, which is commonly\nadopted for superficial lesions (cherry-picking surgery). Less\ncommonly, this procedure can be performed even for more\ncomplex procedures involving right hepatectomy with PSS for\nleft-sided lesions with or without PVE. Torzilli et al. [49,50] ex\u00ad\ntended the PSS to deep-seated lesions (including those in contact\nwith major intrahepatic vessels) and introduced the concept of\n\u201cextended OSH,\u201d thereby using PVE only in exceptional situa\u00ad\ntions. Imperative technical prerequisites include the use of in\u00ad\ntraoperative ultrasound to guide parenchymal resection. Vascular\ncontact with major blood vessels defines marginally resectable\nCRLM, a subset of BR-CRLM. Once objective response is achieved\nby systemic chemotherapy, liver surgeons are left with 2 options:\nR1 resection by necessity [51] or liver resection with vascular\nreconstruction. R1 resection because of vascular contact is fea\u00ad\nsible and may achieve results comparable with R0 resection,\nwhich is not the case for parenchymal R1 resection [52]. This\nfinding may be dependent on tumor biology, particularly _KRAS_\nmutation [53]. Various parenchymal-sparing techniques that\nemploy R1 vascular resection have been described with accep\u00ad\ntable oncologically outcomes [54]. Some preoperative parameters\nmay lead surgeons to prefer radical resections with vascular re\u00ad\nconstruction rather than PSS (eg, contact of > 25% of the vessel\ncircumference, which is associated with a higher risk of vessel\ninvasion) [55]. However, a lack of technical expertise and a higher\nrisk of postoperative morbidity, including posthepatectomy liver\nfailure, are deterrents to its widespread applicability.\n5. Liver transplantation: Despite the surgical advances and effective\nchemotherapy, many patients with CRLM remain unresectable.\nAlthough not considered for BR-CRLM, liver transplantation re\u00ad\nmains a viable option for patients who remain unresectable.\n\n5\n\n_G.P. HAIC has the potential to act synergistically with systemic\nchemotherapy to achieve resectability in cases of initially\nunresectable CRLM. The\nsuccessful resectability achieved in this retrospective study was\nfurther replicated in prospective trials conducted by the same\ncenter using strict definitions to categorize patients as initially\nunresectable CRLM [59,60]. There are currently enough data to\nsupport the use of HAIC along with systemic therapy to produce\nresponse rates higher than systemic chemotherapy alone. For patients\nwho have failed multiple lines of chemotherapy, HAIC has shown\nbenefit in terms of achieving superior response rates. Another role of\nHAIC in patients with BR-CRLM is in the adjuvant setting after a\nsuccessful hepatic resection to decrease the incidence of recurrence in\nthe remnant liver. Before instituting HAIC, surgical placement of a\nhepatic artery infusion (HAI) catheter and pump is required,\ncommonly in the gastroduodenal artery after delineating the arterial\nanatomy of the liver via cross-sectional imaging. Complications\nassociated with HAI pump placement are primarily related to the\ncatheter, such as arterial thrombosis, whereas systemic complications\nassociated with extrahepatic perfusion are rare.\n\n_TACE_ . TACE is a widely used and accepted treatment modality in\nunresectable hepatocellular carcinoma. Conventional TACE and its\nrelatively newer technology of drug-eluting beads (DEB-TACE)\nconsisting of a novel drug delivery system have been investigated in\nCRLM. After its successful application in the chemotherapy-refractory\nunresectable CRLM, its utility was explored in the neoadjuvant setting\nto achieve resectability in patients who were deemed as initially\nunresectable. A multicenter RCT comparing DEB-TACE with irinotecan\nbeads (DEBIRI-TACE) with systemic chemotherapy with systemic\nchemotherapy alone in unresectable CRLM found higher response\nrates, longer PFS (15.2 vs 7.6, respectively), and greater conversion to\nhepatic resection in the combination group [61]. Of 40\npatients recruited in the trial who underwent preoperative DEBIRITACE, 38 (95.0%) underwent resection with an R0 resection rate of\n74.0% [62]. Nonetheless, this technique should still be considered as a\nsecondary treatment option in CRLM and more so in BR-CRLM.\n\n_Ablation techniques_\nRFA, MWA, and the relatively novel technique of irreversible\nelectroporation (IRE) are the ablation modalities tried and tested in\nCRLM. A major limitation of RFA is the heat sink effect, which occurs\nwhen a tumor abuts a blood vessel of a size of > 3 mm. This limita\u00ad\ntion is somewhat circumvented with MWA, which produces more\nheat. However, IRE is not impeded by the heat sink effect of neigh\u00ad\nboring blood vessels.
